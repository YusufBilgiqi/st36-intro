01 — History

From Handmade to Mass Production

Understanding how watch manufacturing evolved explains why modern watches are designed the way they are — and why they're so much easier to service than their ancestors.

Pre-1850s
The Handmade Era
Every part individually fitted by a skilled craftsman. Parts were not interchangeable — a replacement had to be made and hand-fitted each time. Beautiful, but slow and expensive to make and repair. English and high-grade Swiss work fell into this category.
Mid-1800s onward
Early Mass Production (American Lead)
Swiss and American factories adopted machine production. Parts were made to tolerances tight enough that assemblers could pick components from bins and fit them together with minimal adjustment. Cheaper, faster, and more reliable for repairs — but early tolerances still varied by manufacturer quality.
Early 20th century
Decline of Handmade Watches
Affordable American imports put handmade English and high-cost Swiss watches under fatal commercial pressure. The handmade tradition declined sharply. English watchmaking never fully recovered.
Today
Modern Precision — Full Interchangeability
Manufacturing tolerances are extremely precise even in budget movements. An escape wheel from one movement will drop straight into another of the same caliber. This is the foundation of modern servicing — you can order parts and fit them without hand-adjustment.
For beginners: Handmade movements (English fusee watches, early Swiss lever watches) are not recommended starting points. The hand-fitting required is a specialist skill. Modern mass-produced movements like the ST36 are far more forgiving and practical to learn on.
02 — Construction Types

How Movements Are Constructed

The physical layout of plates, bridges, and cocks determines how easy a movement is to assemble, service, and work on. Construction evolved significantly across the 19th and 20th centuries.

🏛️

Full Plate

Two flat plates separated by pillars — like a miniature clock. The balance sat outside the plates under an ornate cover. Common until late 19th century. Thick, complex to assemble (all pivots must align simultaneously), and hard to service.

3/4

¾ Plate

A quarter of the top plate is cut away, allowing the balance and its cock to sit inside the movement. Thinner than full plate. Common in size 16 pocket watches and smaller. The balance cock and top plate sit flush, requiring a precise cutout.

🔩

Bridge & Cock (Modern)

The top plate is replaced entirely by individual bridges and cocks. Each bridge or cock supports one to four arbors. Mounted on feet, precisely located by steady pins. The dominant method in all 20th and 21st century watches.

⚙️ Bridges vs. Cocks — What's the Difference?

Why modern construction is better for watchmakers: With bridges and cocks, you assemble and test the movement in stages — fit the barrel, check it runs; add the train, check again; add the escapement. With full-plate construction you had to drop all pivots into their holes simultaneously. One mistake meant starting over.
03 — The ST36 Movement

The ST36 — A Modern ETA 6497 Clone

The ST36 is a clone of the ETA 6497 — a large, hand-wound pocket watch movement popular for its accessibility and excellent build quality. It's an ideal learning movement: big enough to work on without a microscope, well-documented, and robust.

Movement Dimensions
Diameter: 16½ lignes ≈ 36.60 mm
Height: ≈ 4.5 mm (main plate bottom to highest point)
Frequency: 18,000 VPH (2.5 Hz)
Power reserve: ~46 hours

Balance cock — supports balance wheel (one end only = cock)
Steady pins: Bridges are not just screwed down — they're also located by two steady (or locating) pins pressed into the main plate. These ensure each bridge sits in exactly the same position every time, maintaining perfect pivot alignment. You can feel them engage with a satisfying click during reassembly.

💎 Jewel Count — 17 Jewels Explained

A "fully jewelled" watch has at least 15 jewels. The ST36 has 17. Each jewel serves a specific purpose — reducing friction and wear at a critical bearing point.

How jewels are fitted: Train wheel jewels are friction-fit — pressed directly into the plate. Pallet jewels and the impulse pin are secured with shellac (heat-softened natural resin). Balance staff jewels are pressed into shock-resistant chatons (Incabloc system) so they can flex and spring back on impact, protecting the delicate balance staff pivots.
Plate material: Main plate, bridges, and cocks are made from nickel-plated brass or a similar copper-based alloy. The finish (engine-turned on American watches, plain brushed on Japanese/Chinese/lower Swiss) is purely cosmetic — it has absolutely no effect on how the movement performs.

1st Wheel — Barrel

Houses the mainspring and barrel arbor. The first wheel of the power train. In this 30-hour movement, the barrel drives the center wheel directly — no intermediate wheel needed. The teeth on the barrel wall mesh with the center wheel pinion.

⏱️

2nd Wheel — Center

Directly driven by the barrel. Completes one revolution per hour — this arbor extends through the front plate to carry the cannon pinion and minute hand. Often sits at the geometric center of the movement.

🔧

3rd Wheel

Driven by the center wheel pinion. In the ST36, part of the third pinion is machined away to provide clearance for the fourth wheel to nest alongside it — an elegant space-saving solution common in this layout.

🔴

4th Wheel

Completes one revolution per minute — the front pivot carries the seconds hand, positioned at the 9 o'clock sub-dial. The 4th wheel is the key stage that steps up the train speed enough for the high-frequency escapement.

⚡

Escape Wheel

Unlike all the other wheels, the escape wheel is made from hardened steel (not brass). Its teeth are bevelled on the underside to reduce contact area with the pallet jewels — both reducing friction and acting as an oil retention feature similar to an oil sink.

05 — Barrel Assembly

The Barrel & Mainspring

The ST36 uses a standard going barrel with resilient hooking — the most common mainspring arrangement in modern watches. Here's everything you need to know to service it correctly.

The "Snailed" Barrel Arbor
The barrel arbor has a hook at its centre for the mainspring eye. The arbor is described as "snailed" — in practice this usually means a flat (not spiral) profile is machined onto it, rather than a true snail shape. This profile allows the spring to conform closely to the arbor, reducing distortion and ensuring smooth energy transfer.
Resilient Hooking (Outer End)
At the outer end of the spring, the resilient hooking fits against a step in the barrel wall. Unlike a simple hook-and-eye, resilient hooking allows the spring to flex slightly, absorbing shock and reducing the risk of breakage. This is the standard arrangement in modern manual-wind watch movements.
To open or not to open? If the barrel cover has no slot for removal, this sometimes signals the barrel should not be opened — replace as a complete unit instead. On automatic watches, the barrel may have "DO NOT OPEN" printed on it. The ST36 barrel cover can be removed safely for cleaning and spring replacement.
Fitting a new mainspring: New springs come in a keeper (a small cup). Place the keeper over the barrel, verify the spring is the right way up, and press it in. New springs come pre-lubricated — do not add more lubricant. For removal, ease the spring out from the centre with brass tweezers, letting it expand into your palm in a controlled way. Wear eye protection. For refitting, always use a mainspring winder to avoid coning or distortion.
06 — Motion Work

Motion Work — Driving the Hands

The motion work sits under the dial and converts the center wheel's one-revolution-per-hour rotation into both the minute hand (1 rev/hour) and the hour hand (1 rev/12 hours). This requires a precise 12:1 gear reduction in two stages.

🕐 The 12:1 Reduction in Two Stages

Stage 1 — 3:1
Cannon pinion (e.g. 12 leaves) meshes with the intermediate/minute wheel (e.g. 36 teeth).

36 ÷ 12 = 3 → for every 3 turns of the cannon pinion, the minute wheel turns once.
Stage 2 — 4:1
Minute wheel pinion (e.g. 8–10 leaves) meshes with the hour wheel (e.g. 32–40 teeth).

32 ÷ 8 = 4 → for every 4 turns of the minute pinion, the hour wheel turns once.
The friction drive (cannon pinion): The cannon pinion is not rigidly fixed to the center arbor — it's a friction (snap) fit. Small indentations ("nips") inside the cannon pinion press against the back taper of the arbor, providing just enough grip to turn the hands during normal running. When setting the time, the cannon pinion slips around the arbor without transmitting force back into the train — protecting the escapement.
07 — Keyless Work

The Keyless Winding & Setting Mechanism

The keyless work is the system that lets you wind the mainspring and set the hands using the crown — all without a key. Before the mid-19th century, both tasks required a separate key. Understanding the mechanism is essential because you must remove the stem before disassembling a movement.

Ratchet action: The crown can be turned backward (the familiar clicking sound) without unwinding the spring. When turned backward, the ratchet teeth on the sliding and winding pinions push the sliding pinion left, compressing the yoke spring slightly and slipping past. Forward motion re-engages the teeth. The mainspring itself is protected by the click/recoiling click on the ratchet wheel.

Crown pulled OUT. The setting lever's nib engages the stem groove, pivoting the lever. The lever pushes the yoke LEFT. The sliding pinion disengages from the winding pinion and instead meshes with the intermediate setting wheel.

No ratchet in setting mode: In hand-setting position, there is no ratchet action — the hands can be moved in both directions freely. This lets you set the time accurately by moving the hands backwards if you overshoot.

Removing the Stem

Procedure
1. Locate the setting lever screw
2. Unscrew it approximately one turn (not all the way out)
3. The setting lever moves away from the stem groove
4. The stem can now be withdrawn freely

The setting lever screw doubles as the pivot for the setting lever — it must remain in the movement.
Important Details
The setting lever screw has a flange that seats in recesses in both the main plate and barrel bridge — it's captive and won't fall out when unscrewed one turn.

The threaded end is shouldered — it screws down firmly against the setting lever when tightened. When tightened, the lever and screw rotate together as a unit.
Crown wheel screw — left-hand thread: The crown wheel screw uses a left-hand thread. This is intentional — during winding, the friction forces on the screw act in the tightening direction (for a left-hand thread), preventing it from working loose. Turn it clockwise to loosen.
Recoiling click: The click on the ratchet wheel is a recoiling click — it allows the mainspring to unwind very slightly when winding stops, preventing the spring from being over-tightened. An over-tightened spring drives the balance at excessively high amplitude, causing the impulse pin to strike the outside of the lever horns — a condition called "banking." This makes the watch gain dramatically and can damage the escapement.
08 — Escapement & Regulator

The Escapement & Balance

The vast majority of watches you'll encounter use a club tooth lever escapement — the most reliable and common escapement design ever made. The ST36 is no exception.

The Escapement's Role
The escapement does two things simultaneously:

1. Controls the rate at which the mainspring energy is released — one tooth of the escape wheel is let through per vibration of the balance.

2. Gives the balance a push (impulse) each vibration to keep it swinging — without this the balance would stop from friction.
The Pallets
The pallet fork has two jewel stones (entry and exit pallets) that alternately catch and release the escape wheel teeth. The fork is connected to the balance by the impulse pin on the balance roller.

The two pallet jewels and the impulse pin are all secured with shellac — not friction-fit. This is important when cleaning — shellac dissolves in alcohol-based solutions.
The Regulator
The ST36 has an extended regulator with graduations marked on the balance cock — allowing very precise rate adjustment without a timing machine. The regulator pins move along the hairspring to change its effective length, adjusting the vibration rate and thus the timekeeping rate.

Older watches may just have a "F/S" (fast/slow) scale. The graduated scale on the ST36 is more useful for fine adjustment.
Pivot Types in the ST36
The ST36 uses two pivot types:

Straight pivots — for all train wheels and the pallet staff. End shake controlled by the shoulder bearing on the plate/jewel.

Conical pivots — for the balance staff only. The angled shoulder stops oil migrating up the arbor, and the absence of a sharp root corner makes the pivot far more resistant to shock breakage.

🎯 Final Check — Test Your Understanding

1. You pull the crown out to set the time. What physically moves to shift the sliding pinion into the setting position?

✓ Correct! The stem groove engages the setting lever nib → the lever pivots → its lower end pushes the yoke left → the sliding pinion shifts into the setting position.

2. Why does the crown wheel screw have a left-hand thread?

✓ Correct! This is a clever engineering solution — the forces generated during winding act in the direction that tightens the screw, rather than loosening it. Remember to turn it clockwise to loosen.

3. What is the purpose of the recoiling click on the ratchet wheel?

✓ Correct! Over-tightening the mainspring drives the balance at excessively high amplitude, causing the impulse pin to knock the lever against the banking pins — rapidly gaining rate and risking escapement damage. The recoil prevents this.
